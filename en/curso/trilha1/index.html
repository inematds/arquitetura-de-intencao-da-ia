🔄 The Turning Point: From Operator to Architect

The shift in the question that turns an AI user into a solution builder.

0 of 6
What it is:

The course’s central turning point: stop asking “What prompt should I use?” and start asking “What system do I need to build to solve this problem?”

Why learn:

The question sets the ceiling for the outcome. People who think only in terms of prompts solve isolated tasks; people who think in terms of systems solve entire problems.

Key concepts:

Operator × architect; problem × task; the question as a compass for the solution.

What it is:

Before creating agents or automations, you build the foundation where AI will operate: the way of thinking (mental), the terrain (technical), and the direction (strategic).

Why learn:

Skipping this foundation is mistake #1: it creates polished automations that solve nothing because they have nothing to build on.

Key concepts:

The base's three layers; build the environment before the tool.

What it is:

An AI solution is more than a little chat box. It’s a system with intent, identity, channels, services, security, tools, and continuous improvement.

Why learn:

Treating everything as “a chatbot” limits ambition. Seeing it as a system opens the door to real solutions connected to the business.

Key concepts:

Living system; components; the whole is greater than the sum of its parts.

What it is:

They’re not a traditional programmer or a decorator of tools. They’re someone who understands problems, structures intentions, organizes information, and builds practical solutions for real businesses.

Why learn:

It's the most valuable and scarce role: the bridge between a company's problem and what AI can do.

Key concepts:

Solution builder; clarity as the first skill; translating problem→system.

What it is:

Look at a company and spot opportunities: repetition, wasted time, disorganized information, poor customer service, or slow decisions.

Why learn:

It’s the radar that turns “I want to use AI” into “I’m going to solve this specific, costly problem.”

Key concepts:

Map of opportunities; repetition and friction; multiply results.

What it is:

Without architecture, AI degrades: without intent, it becomes trial and error; without context, it becomes guesswork; without rules, it becomes a risk; without a channel, it becomes an isolated tool; without measurement, it becomes an illusion of productivity.

Why learn:

Recognizing each gap shows exactly which piece of the architecture a project is missing.

Key concepts:

Intent, context, rule, channel, measurement — the five minimum pillars.

🤖 The Jarvis Metaphor

The mental image that guides the entire course: a system with a soul, layers, limits, and an intention at its core.

0 of 7
What it is:

Jarvis is a metaphor for a complete AI solution: an entity that operates with purpose, rather than merely responding to messages.

Why learn:

The metaphor gives you a single mental model that organizes all the components we’ll cover in the course.

Key concepts:

Entity × tool; operate × respond; the system as a character.

What it is:

The core of the solution: what the system is, why it exists, what problem it solves, who it works for, and what it can and cannot do.

Why learn:

Without a soul, the system is a pile of disconnected tools. With a soul, it becomes a coherent solution.

Key concepts:

Identity; purpose; audience; the core that gives meaning to the parts.

What it is:

Jarvis’s anatomy: channels (input/output), services (capability blocks), agents (workers), and skills (what they can do).

Why learn:

Knowing the layers gives you the vocabulary to design any solution in an organized way.

Key concepts:

Layered view; separation of responsibilities; system map.

What it is:

Jarvis has guardrails: rules, permissions, action limits, and validations that prevent the system from doing the wrong things.

Why learn:

When AI performs real tasks, safety stops being a technical detail and becomes part of the architecture.

Key concepts:

Limits; permissions; security as architecture, not decoration.

What it is:

Tools are the resources Jarvis uses to take action: spreadsheets, CRM, database, calendar, messaging, APIs, payments.

Why learn:

The tool never comes before the intent—it serves a result. Reversing that creates a “solution looking for a problem.”

Key concepts:

A tool serves intent; action capabilities; connection to the real world.

What it is:

Jarvis remembers context and gets better over time: it learns, adapts, and evolves as it receives feedback and new data.

Why learn:

Without memory and evolution, the solution freezes. With them, it becomes an asset that improves on its own.

Key concepts:

Memory; adaptation; continuous improvement.

What it is:

Above all, Jarvis has an intent—the destination that guides every decision, channel, agent, and tool.

Why learn:

Without a destination, any automation seems good and any answer seems sufficient. Intent is the compass.

Key concepts:

Intent as destination; coherence; everything in service of the purpose.

📁 Infrastructure: Folders, Terminal, and Version Control

Lose your fear of infrastructure: organize projects, use the terminal, and version with Git/GitHub.

0 of 6
What it is:

Know how to organize folders, files, projects, and versions—the physical foundation where every solution lives.

Why learn:

A disorganized project becomes chaos. Clear structure is the first sign of an architect.

Key concepts:

Folder structure; naming conventions; organization by project.

What it is:

Understand the terminal—not to become a command-line expert, but to feel less intimidated by the infrastructure.

Why learn:

The terminal is the gateway to servers, deploys, and tools. Fear of it means dependence on third parties.

Key concepts:

Basic commands; navigation; familiarity, not complete mastery.

What it is:

GitHub stores versions, history, and project organization. Every solution needs version control.

Why learn:

Version control protects your work, lets you roll back, and opens the door to collaboration and deployment.

Key concepts:

Repository; commit; history; living backup.

What it is:

Version control is also collaboration: several people working on the same project without getting in each other's way, with a history of who changed what.

Why learn:

Real solutions are rarely built alone—and history is the project’s memory.

Key concepts:

Collaboration; history; traceability.

What it is:

You don’t need to master everything like a senior engineer—but you do need to understand the infrastructure map.

Why learn:

Those who don’t understand the map always depend on someone to make the journey.

Key concepts:

Map × mastery; autonomy; knowing enough to decide.

What it is:

The minimum set of tools an architect works with: an editor, terminal, GitHub account, and the habit of staying organized.

Why learn:

An environment set up once saves hours on every project that follows.

Key concepts:

Setup; editor; organizational habits.

🌐 Publish to the World

Getting a solution up and running: hosting, VPS, server, domain, database, API, deploy, and separating test from production.

0 of 7
What it is:

Publishing means putting a solution out into the world: making it accessible so people and systems can actually use it.

Why learn:

A solution that only runs on your machine doesn’t solve anyone’s problem. Publishing is what makes it real.

Key concepts:

Location × audience; accessibility; “live.”

What it is:

The simplest way to publish: host a static site or page on ready-made services.

Why learn:

It's the first possible "live" version—fast, inexpensive, and enough for many prototypes.

Key concepts:

Hosting; static site; quick publishing.

What it is:

A VPS is a cloud server where you run services that need to stay online all the time — like a Jarvis.

Why learn:

When the solution grows beyond a website, a VPS provides control and continuity.

Key concepts:

Server; cloud; always-on process.

What it is:

The domain is an easy-to-remember address that points to where the solution is hosted.

Why learn:

It's the solution's public and professional identity—the name people use to find it.

Key concepts:

Mastery; DNS (overview); public address.

What it is:

A database stores information; an API is how systems communicate; a webhook is an automatic notification when something happens.

Why learn:

These are the connectors that link Jarvis to the rest of the world—understanding the concept is enough to design the workflow.

Key concepts:

Database; API; webhook; integration.

What it is:

Deploy is the act of taking the current version of the solution to the environment where it actually runs.

Why learn:

It's the step that connects "it's ready on my computer" with "it's working for the customer."

Key concepts:

Deploy; publish a version; from code to live.

What it is:

A test environment is where you experiment without risk; production is where the customer actually uses it. They stay separate.

Why learn:

Mixing testing and production is one of the biggest sources of accidents—and it's also a safety rule.

Key concepts:

Testing × production; safe environment; risk separation.

🚪 Input and Output Channels

How the world talks to Jarvis and how it responds: the channel is the entry point for intent.

0 of 6
What it is:

The channel is how the world talks to Jarvis and how it responds. It's the gateway for incoming intentions.

Why learn:

Without a channel, the system is an island. The channel connects the customer’s intent to the right service.

Key concepts:

Channel; gateway; input and output.

What it is:

The conversational channels most used by businesses: WhatsApp, Telegram, and website chat.

Why learn:

It’s where the customer already is. Meeting customers on their channel reduces friction to almost zero.

Key concepts:

Conversational channels; where the customer is.

What it is:

Beyond chat, Jarvis can receive and respond via email, an internal dashboard, an app, voice, a form, or API.

Why learn:

Each channel serves a context. Choosing the right channel is part of designing the solution.

Key concepts:

Multichannel; usage context; the right channel for the task.

What it is:

When a customer sends a message, they’re not just writing text: they’re bringing an intention (to buy, resolve an issue, schedule, complain).

Why learn:

The system needs to capture the intent behind the message—not just the words.

Key concepts:

Intent × text; understand what the client really wants.

What it is:

Once the intent is captured, the system interprets the context and activates the right service (sales, support, scheduling...).

Why learn:

It's the routing intelligence that connects the channel to internal services—the heart of the flow.

Key concepts:

Interpretation; routing; intent → service.

What it is:

The channel works both ways: the intention comes in through it, and Jarvis's response goes out through it, completing the communication cycle.

Why learn:

Thinking about input and output together prevents solutions that understand the customer but don't know how to respond well.

Key concepts:

Two-way; cycle; response as part of the channel.
